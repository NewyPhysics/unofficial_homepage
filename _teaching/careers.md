A physics degree doesn't lead to just one job. Physicists have a habit of turning up everywhere: in banks and hospitals, at the front of classrooms, and occasionally in the cockpit of a fighter jet. It trains you to understand how things work, to model complex problems with mathematics, to write code, to design experiments and to make sense of messy data. Those skills are valued almost everywhere, which is why physics graduates go on to an unusually wide range of careers.

## Skills you'll graduate with

- **Problem solving:** breaking complex, unfamiliar problems down and working through them from first principles.
- **Mathematical modelling:** describing real systems with equations, and knowing when an approximation is good enough.
- **Programming and data analysis:** writing code, running simulations and analysing large datasets, often in Python.
- **Experimental skills:** designing measurements, working with instruments and lasers, and understanding uncertainty.
- **Communication:** explaining technical work clearly in reports, presentations and conversations.

## Physics or engineering: which pays more?

It's a fair question, and the answer is: not by as much as you might think. Here are national figures for 2024 graduates, about four to six months after finishing their degree:

| | Median starting salary | In full-time work |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering | $80,000 | 86% |
| Science and mathematics | $72,400 | 64% |
| All fields | $75,000 | 74% |
{: .salary-table}

Engineers start a few thousand dollars ahead, but those figures don't tell the whole story for physics:

- **Physics is grouped with all the sciences.** The national figures lump physics in with fields that typically pay less; physics on its own isn't reported.
- **Many physics graduates are still studying.** Lots of them go straight into Honours or a PhD, so they don't count as employed in the first few months. At Newcastle, 38.9% of science and mathematics graduates go on to full-time study, and 88.5% are in some form of employment.
- **The gap closes.** Within three years, the share of science graduates in full-time work rises by about 25 percentage points, and many physics graduates move into data science, finance and software, where salaries often overtake engineering.
- **Starting pay goes further in the Hunter.** Graduate salaries here are similar to Sydney's, and the cost of living is lower.

<p class="salary-note">National figures are from the 2024 Graduate Outcomes Survey (QILT) for undergraduate graduates. For University of Newcastle figures, see <a href="https://www.compared.edu.au/institution/university-of-newcastle/study-area/science-mathematics/undergraduate">graduate outcomes for Newcastle science and mathematics graduates on ComparED</a>.</p>

## Growing areas for physicists

- **Medical physics:** working in hospitals on radiation therapy, imaging and radiation safety. Becoming a clinical medical physicist usually involves further postgraduate study and clinical training. Our own medical physics research group works alongside the radiation oncology team at Calvary Mater Newcastle.
- **Space and quantum technology:** fast-growing Australian industries, from satellites and space weather to quantum sensing and computing.
- **Renewable energy:** solar, storage and grid technologies, in the Hunter region and beyond.
- **Research:** after Honours and a PhD, graduates do research at universities and at organisations such as CSIRO, ANSTO and the Bureau of Meteorology.
